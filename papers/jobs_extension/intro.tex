% ==============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ==============================================================================

Maintenance hangars are a scarce and tightly constrained resource.  An
aircraft parked at a \emph{front} position physically blocks the access
path of any aircraft parked at the \emph{rear} positions behind it, so
servicing the rear aircraft may require temporarily towing the front one
out of the way and returning it once the manoeuvre is complete.  Each such
manoeuvre occupies the ground crew, interrupts ongoing service, and carries
a non-negligible risk of damage, so an operator wants to schedule the fleet
to finish on time while keeping makespan and the number of manoeuvres low.

A companion paper modelled this problem at the \emph{aircraft level},
treating each aircraft as a single uninterrupted block of occupancy.  That
abstraction is adequate when a blocking interaction can only be resolved by
serialising the two aircraft, but it discards the operational reality that
the work performed on an aircraft is a \emph{chain of jobs} with individual
durations, and that some of those jobs can be paused briefly to clear the
way for a neighbouring movement while others cannot.  Whether a rear
aircraft can be accessed while a front aircraft is being serviced depends
precisely on \emph{which job} the front aircraft is running at that
instant: an access that falls in an inter-job gap is cheap, one that falls
inside an interruptible job costs a bounded extension, and one that falls
inside a non-interruptible job is simply impossible.  Capturing these
distinctions requires modelling blocking at the \emph{job level}, which is
the subject of this paper.

We make two contributions.  First, we give an exact mixed-integer linear
programming (MILP) formulation of the job-level problem
(\S\ref{sec:milp}).  Its central device is a partition constraint that
classifies every access of a blocked aircraft into exactly one of four
mutually exclusive states --- before the front stay, after it, in an
inter-job gap (Mode~B), or strictly inside an interruptible job (Mode~C)
--- so that an access which cannot be classified, because its blocker is
mid-way through a non-interruptible job, is correctly rendered infeasible.
Second, we develop a metaheuristic, \textsc{IGVND} (\S\ref{sec:heuristic}),
that couples an Iterated-Greedy outer loop with a Variable-Neighbourhood-%
Descent local search around a problem-specific decoder, separating the
combinatorial assignment-and-ordering decision from the timing decision and
pricing the former with a fast deterministic schedule builder.

We then compare the two methods on a controlled 270-instance benchmark that
spans five blocking topologies, four fleet sizes and three slack levels,
under three weight profiles that each isolate one objective term
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}).  The exact solver plays a dual role: it is the
\emph{yardstick} that certifies the heuristic's quality on the instances it
can solve to optimality, and the \emph{baseline} it competes against on the
instances it cannot.  The comparison shows that IGVND is exact or
near-exact wherever the MILP proves optimality, and that it delivers
substantially better feasible solutions in the same time budget on every
configuration the MILP cannot close --- which is the regime an operator
faces at realistic scale.
