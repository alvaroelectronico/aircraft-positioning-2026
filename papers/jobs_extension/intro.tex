% ==============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ==============================================================================

Keeping a commercial aircraft fleet airworthy requires a continual
programme of scheduled maintenance, most of which is carried out in hangars
whose parking capacity is limited and costly.  A hangar's parking positions
are therefore a scarce and tightly constrained resource, and their physical
layout adds a further complication: an aircraft parked at a \emph{front}
position physically blocks the access path of any aircraft parked at the
\emph{rear} positions behind it, so servicing the rear aircraft may require
temporarily towing the front one out of the way and returning it once the
manoeuvre is complete.  Each such manoeuvre occupies the ground crew,
interrupts ongoing service, and carries a non-negligible risk of damage, so
an operator wants to schedule the fleet to finish on time while keeping
makespan and the number of manoeuvres low.

The work performed on an aircraft is not monolithic: it is a \emph{chain of
jobs} with individual durations, and some of those jobs can be paused
briefly to clear the way for a neighbouring movement while others cannot.
Whether a rear aircraft can be accessed while a front aircraft is being
serviced therefore depends precisely on \emph{which job} the front aircraft
is running at that instant: an access that falls in an inter-job gap is
cheap, one that falls inside an interruptible job costs a bounded
extension, and one that falls inside a non-interruptible job is simply
impossible.  These distinctions determine both the feasibility and the cost
of every manoeuvre, so treating them faithfully is essential --- and it is
what this paper sets out to do.

The problem combines two classical strands of the scheduling literature.
From machine scheduling with blocking~\citep{HallSriskandarajah1996,
MascisPacciarelli2002} it inherits the defining trait that a scheduled
entity can deny a resource to others even while no work is being performed
on it; from resource-constrained project
scheduling~\citep{Blazewicz1983} it takes the precedence-linked chain of
jobs that each aircraft carries.  It differs, however, from the spatial
scheduling formulations used for hangar planning, which couple geometric
non-overlap --- No-Fit Polygons, safety distances, or taxi-route
capacities --- to the start-time decisions~\citep{Qin2019, Pazhooh2025}:
here the geometry is compiled once into a fixed directed blocking graph
over positions, and the interference it induces is temporal and
task-dependent --- it binds only at the two access instants of the rear
aircraft, with a severity that depends on which job the front aircraft is
running at those instants.

We make two contributions.  First, we give an exact mixed-integer linear
programming (MILP) formulation of the problem
(\S\ref{sec:milp}).  Its central device is a partition constraint that
classifies every access of a blocked aircraft into exactly one of four
mutually exclusive states --- before the front stay, after it, in an
inter-job gap (Mode~B), or strictly inside an interruptible job (Mode~C)
--- so that an access which cannot be classified, because its blocker is
mid-way through a non-interruptible job, is correctly rendered infeasible.
Second, we develop a metaheuristic, \textsc{IGVND} (\S\ref{sec:heuristic}),
that couples an Iterated-Greedy outer loop with a Variable-Neighbourhood-%
Descent local search around a problem-specific decoder, separating the
combinatorial assignment-and-ordering decision from the timing decision and
pricing the former with a fast deterministic schedule builder.

We then compare the two methods on a controlled 270-instance benchmark that
spans five blocking topologies, four fleet sizes and three slack levels,
under three weight profiles that each isolate one objective term
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}).  The exact solver plays a dual role: it is the
\emph{yardstick} that certifies the heuristic's quality on the instances it
can solve to optimality, and the \emph{baseline} it competes against on the
instances it cannot.  The comparison shows that IGVND is exact or
near-exact wherever the MILP proves optimality, and that it delivers
substantially better feasible solutions in the same time budget on every
configuration the MILP cannot close --- which is the regime an operator
faces at realistic scale.
