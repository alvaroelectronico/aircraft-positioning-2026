\section{Computational experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ==============================================================================

This section evaluates IGVND on a controlled benchmark.  The reference
is the MILP of Section~\ref{sec:milp} solved with Gurobi under the same time
limit: a proven optimum where the solver converges, and its best
incumbent otherwise.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Benchmark design}
\label{sec:benchmark}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The benchmark isolates three axes of difficulty: blocking topology,
fleet size $R$ and time-window slack (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}).  All
instances use $P = 5$ positions.  Four topologies are considered:
\emph{None} ($\calA = \emptyset$), \emph{Chain}
($\calA = \{(p_i, p_{i+1})\}$, a single-lane hangar), \emph{Hub} (one
aisle position blocks all others) and \emph{Two rows} (front and rear
halves paired position by position).  \emph{None} is evaluated only at
$R = 10$ with tight windows, as the no-blocking control; each blocking
topology is crossed with $R \in \{5, 10, 20, 30\}$ and three slack levels
(loose, medium, tight).  The 37 configurations are each replicated with
10 independently seeded instances, giving 370 instances.  Processing
times, earliest starts and due dates are sampled on an integer-day grid;
the tow time is $\varepsilon = 0.5$ days.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Both methods are run on every instance under three weight profiles that
each prioritise one objective term: makespan-priority
$w^{\mathrm{MK}} = (100, 1, 1)$, delay-priority
$w^{\mathrm{DLY}} = (1, 100, 1)$, and movement-priority
$w^{\mathrm{MOV}} = (1, 1, 100)$, for $(W^M, W^D, W^S)$.  Each run is given
a strictly enforced wall-clock budget of 60\,s.  We report the relative
objective gap
\[
  g = \frac{z_{\mathrm{MILP}} - z_{\mathrm{IGVND}}}{z_{\mathrm{MILP}}},
\]
so that $g > 0$ means IGVND attains the lower (better) objective on this
minimisation problem.  The relative gap is inflated by small denominators
when the reference objective is near zero, which happens under the
delay-priority profile whenever the optimal delay is close to zero; we
therefore also report the absolute differences in each objective
component (IGVND $-$ MILP) for makespan, delay and movement count, and
refer to them wherever a relative gap is driven by a small denominator.
Both quantities are aggregated as the mean over the 10 seeds of each
configuration.  Every IGVND solution is validated by the independent
feasibility checker as it is produced, before being scored; across the
full $370 \times 3 = 1110$ heuristic runs there were no feasibility
failures.  IGVND is implemented in Python; its two decoders, the
neighbourhood scan, the reinsertion step and the alignment step are
compiled with Numba.  The heuristic runs reported here were executed three at a
time on a laptop (Intel i7-11390H, 4 cores, 16\,GB RAM, Windows~11,
Python~3.14, Numba~0.68).

Table~\ref{tab:milp_conv} reports the optimality gap of the reference at
the end of the budget.  The reference is a proven optimum on every
five-aircraft configuration, on the no-blocking control and on most
loose $R{=}10$ configurations, 439 instance--profile pairs in all; on
the remaining configurations it is a feasible incumbent, so a positive
$g$ there means that IGVND finds a better feasible solution within the
same time, not that it improves on a proven optimum.

On the 439 pairs with a certified optimum, IGVND attains the optimal
objective value in 404, including 267 of the 270 five-aircraft runs and
the no-blocking control under all three profiles.  The remaining
deviations are small in absolute terms: under makespan-priority IGVND
ends within $1.4\%$ of the reference on every $R{=}10$ cell, and the
negative mean gaps of up to $-6.2\%$ under delay-priority correspond to
a mean $\Delta D$ of at most $+0.1$ delay units, inflated by a reference
delay close to zero.  Only three of the 1110 runs end more than $20\%$
above the reference.  Most of the optima that a zero-shift decoding
misses delay a rear aircraft, or stretch its stay, so that its access
coincides with a boundary of a blocking front aircraft; given the
reference assignment and order, the alignment refinement of
Section~\ref{sec:igvnd_align} recovers 413 of the 439 optima from the zero
shift, and without it the two $R{=}10$-loose delay-priority cells had
mean gaps of $-111\%$ and $-148\%$ instead of $-3.8\%$ and $-6.2\%$.

Where the reference is an incumbent, IGVND is better on the tight
$R{=}10$ cells (by $+2.0\%$ to $+16.2\%$ under delay-priority) and within
$1.4\%$ of the reference elsewhere at that size.  From $R{=}20$ it
returns the better feasible solution on every configuration, profile and
seed (540 runs), with relative gaps from $+15.6\%$ to $+39.8\%$ at
$R{=}20$ and from $+20.6\%$ to $+52.9\%$ at $R{=}30$, and absolute
differences of the same sign in makespan and delay throughout.  On
\emph{Chain} at scale, under makespan- and delay-priority, IGVND uses
more access manoeuvres than the reference ($\Delta n$ up to $+47$ at
$R{=}30$) while reducing delay by more than a thousand units.  Under
movement-priority both methods reach a zero-manoeuvre schedule, so the
comparison reduces to how tightly each packs it: IGVND matches the
optimal value on every certified cell, is within $0.5\%$ of the
reference on the remaining $R{=}10$ cells, and improves on it by $+19\%$
to $+45\%$ at $R{=}20$ and $R{=}30$, where the nest-stretch candidate of
Section~\ref{sec:igvnd_safety} supplies the extended rear-aircraft stays
observed in the certified optima.
