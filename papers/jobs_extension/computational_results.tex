\section{Computational Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ==============================================================================

This section compares the IGVND heuristic of \S\ref{sec:heuristic} against
the exact MILP of \S\ref{sec:milp} on a controlled benchmark.  The MILP is
solved with Gurobi and serves as the reference: where it converges it
provides a proven optimum; where it does not, its 60\,s incumbent is the
best exact-solver result obtainable under the same budget.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Benchmark design}
\label{sec:benchmark}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The benchmark isolates three axes of difficulty: the blocking topology, the
fleet size $R$, and the time-window slack.  All instances use $P = 5$
positions.  Five topology families span the spectrum from independent to
constrained hangars: \emph{None} ($\calA = \emptyset$), \emph{Chain}
($\calA = \{(p_i, p_{i+1})\}$), \emph{Hub} (one aisle position blocks all
others), \emph{Triangle} (a transitive triangle on the rear trio, the layout
of the facility that motivated this study), and \emph{Two rows} (front and
rear halves paired position-by-position).  \emph{None}, \emph{Chain} and
\emph{Hub} are evaluated at the baseline ($R = 10$, tight windows) to isolate
the effect of blocking structure.  \emph{Two rows} and \emph{Triangle} are
additionally crossed over four fleet sizes ($R \in \{5, 10, 20, 30\}$) and
three slack levels (loose, medium, tight), so the effects of size and of
time-window tightness can each be read independently.  This gives
\textbf{27 configurations}, each replicated with 10 independently seeded
instances, for \textbf{270 instances} in total (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}).
Processing times, earliest starts and due dates are sampled on an integer-day
grid; the tow time is fixed at $\varepsilon = 0.5$ days.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Both methods are run on every instance under three weight profiles that
each isolate one objective term: makespan-priority
$w^{\mathrm{MK}} = (100, 1, 1)$, delay-priority
$w^{\mathrm{DLY}} = (1, 100, 1)$, and movement-priority
$w^{\mathrm{MOV}} = (1, 1, 100)$, for $(W^M, W^D, W^S)$.  Each run is given
a strictly enforced wall-clock budget of 60\,s.  We report the relative
objective gap
\[
  g = \frac{z_{\mathrm{MILP}} - z_{\mathrm{IGVND}}}{z_{\mathrm{MILP}}},
\]
so that $g > 0$ means IGVND attains the lower (better) objective on this
minimisation problem.  Because the relative gap is distorted by small
denominators when the optimal delay is near zero, we also report the
undistorted per-component differences (IGVND $-$ MILP) for makespan, delay
and movement count.  Both quantities are aggregated as the mean over the
10 seeds of each configuration.  All IGVND solutions are validated by the
independent feasibility checker before being scored; across the full
$270 \times 3 = 810$ heuristic runs there were no feasibility failures ---
in contrast to the MILP, which failed outright on the largest instances
(\S\ref{sec:milp_conv}).

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{MILP convergence}
\label{sec:milp_conv}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:milp_conv} reports the mean optimality gap Gurobi still
reports at the end of the budget, per configuration and profile.  The pattern
frames the entire comparison.  The MILP is reliable as a \emph{proven optimum}
(mean gap $0.0\%$) only on the small and loosely constrained cases: the
five-aircraft instances and the no-blocking baseline close on all profiles in
well under a second, as do the loosely-constrained $R{=}10$ instances.  As
soon as the fleet grows ($R \ge 20$) or the profile activates the manoeuvre
machinery (\emph{delay-} and \emph{movement-priority} couple the timing and
classification binaries far more tightly than makespan-priority), the gap blows
up: it exceeds $60\%$ at $R{=}20$, climbs to around $75$--$80\%$ at $R{=}30$, and reaches
essentially $100\%$ under the delay-priority profile, where Gurobi returns a
feasible incumbent without closing the bound at all.  Makespan-priority is
consistently the easiest profile for the exact solver (smallest gaps),
delay-priority the hardest.

At the largest scale the exact solver does not merely time out: on two of the
$R{=}30$ makespan-priority instances (one \emph{Triangle}-loose, one
\emph{Two rows}-tight, marked $^{(1)}$ in Table~\ref{tab:milp_conv}) Gurobi
\emph{exhausted memory while building the search and returned no solution at
all}.  These are recorded as the MILP being \textbf{unable to solve the
instance} --- a stronger failure than a loose bound --- and are averaged out of
the corresponding cell; the heuristic solved all of them within the 60\,s
budget.  On every configuration with a non-zero gap, a positive $g$ in
Table~\ref{tab:gap_profile} does not mean IGVND beats a proven optimum; it
means IGVND finds a better feasible solution than the exact solver does in the
same time --- or, on those two instances, that it finds a feasible solution at
all where the exact solver cannot.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:gap_profile} reports the relative gap and
Table~\ref{tab:components} the per-component differences.  We read them
together, grouping the configurations by what the MILP can certify.

\paragraph{Where the MILP is a proven optimum, IGVND matches it.}
On the configurations the MILP solves to optimality, IGVND is at or within
a small fraction of a percent of that optimum.  It is \emph{exact} on the
no-blocking baseline and on the five-aircraft instances --- $g = 0.00\%$ on
every $R{=}5$ configuration under the makespan- and movement-priority
profiles, and within $0.3\%$ on average under delay-priority (a single
seed at $-3\%$) --- and within roughly $\pm 2.5\%$ on the remaining
converged $R{=}10$ cases, apart from the delay-priority column on the two
loose $R{=}10$ configurations, whose near-zero delay denominator inflates
the relative gap (discussed next).  The makespan-priority column, where the MILP
converges on most $R{=}10$ topologies, shows IGVND tracking the optimum
closely (\emph{None}, \emph{Triangle}, \emph{Two rows} all within $1\%$);
the small negative entries on \emph{Chain} and \emph{Hub} are at the level
of the search's run-to-run noise.  The corresponding
$\Delta m, \Delta D, \Delta n$ in Table~\ref{tab:components} are
near-zero, confirming that these are genuine near-ties rather than
denominator artefacts.

\paragraph{The apparent delay-priority outliers are small-denominator artefacts.}
The most negative entry in Table~\ref{tab:gap_profile} is the
loosely-constrained triangle under delay-priority ($-33\%$ mean, driven by
one seed at $-277\%$).  Here the MILP drives the total delay to
zero by spending a handful of manoeuvres, so the denominator
$z_{\mathrm{MILP}}$ is tiny and any residual delay explodes the relative
gap: on the extreme seed IGVND concedes about two delay units, and it
attains the strictly better objective on half of the ten seeds.  The
corresponding $\Delta D$ in Table~\ref{tab:components} is essentially zero
($+0.15$ delay units on average):
in absolute terms IGVND loses a negligible amount of delay, not a large
one.  The loose \emph{Two rows} row under the same profile is the mirror
image ($+11.1\%$): IGVND shaves a fraction of a delay unit off a near-zero
optimum, which the tiny denominator turns into a double-digit relative
gain.  Both are exactly the small-denominator inflation the per-component
table is meant to expose.

\paragraph{Where the MILP times out, IGVND wins decisively.}
On the configurations the MILP cannot close in 60\,s --- every $R{=}20$ and
$R{=}30$ case, and the delay-/movement-priority profiles on the tight
$R{=}10$ topologies --- IGVND returns a substantially better feasible
solution within the same budget.  The advantage grows monotonically with
fleet size: the relative gap rises from roughly $+6\%$ to $+21\%$ at
$R{=}20$ to $+18\%$ to $+44\%$ at $R{=}30$, across both the \emph{Two rows}
and \emph{Triangle} families and all three slack levels, peaking under
delay-priority (e.g.\ $+44\%$ on \emph{Triangle}-loose-$R{=}30$).  The
per-component differences confirm a large absolute improvement and not a
denominator effect: $\Delta D$ of several hundred to over a thousand delay
units and $\Delta m$ of tens to over a hundred makespan units, both
negative.  At the extreme, on the two $R{=}30$ instances where Gurobi ran out
of memory the heuristic still returns a fully feasible, checker-validated
schedule.  The mechanism is the architecture of
\S\ref{sec:igvnd_principle}: by searching over the compact combinatorial
state and pricing it with a fast decoder, IGVND evaluates orders of magnitude
more candidate schedules per second than the branch-and-bound search can
while it is still struggling to tighten its bound --- or to fit in memory.

\paragraph{Movement-priority.}
The movement-priority profile is the hardest for a heuristic.  Both methods
almost always reach a zero-manoeuvre schedule ($\Delta n = 0$ throughout), so
the heavily-weighted movement term contributes nothing and the objective
collapses to makespan plus delay; the winner is then simply whichever packs
the zero-movement schedule tightest.  Because that denominator is not small
(it ranges from $\approx 36$ at $R{=}5$ to several thousand at $R{=}30$), the
relative gaps here are genuine and not a small-denominator artefact --- unlike
the delay-priority outlier of the previous paragraph.  IGVND matches the MILP
\emph{exactly} on every five-aircraft configuration ($g = 0.00\%$, all seeds)
and dominates at $R{=}20$ and $R{=}30$ ($+8\%$ to $+36\%$, with large negative
$\Delta m$ and $\Delta D$ --- hundreds of objective units in absolute terms).
The $R{=}10$ blocking topologies, which earlier revisions of the method lost
by up to $-10\%$, are now near-ties or wins: $-0.6\%$ on the tight
\emph{Chain} and $-1.7\%$ on \emph{Hub} (both against an \emph{unconverged}
MILP incumbent, $82\%$ and $32\%$ optimality gaps), and $+0.4\%$ on the loose
\emph{Triangle}, where the MILP \emph{does} prove optimality and IGVND now
attains or beats the certified optimum on eight of ten seeds --- on several
of them ending strictly below the MILP's reported objective, which is
possible because the MILP grids time to integers while the continuous
schedule admits finer starts.  Two ingredients closed these gaps: a
nest-stretch candidate that reproduces the optimum's stay-stretching
mechanism over the actual blocking digraph (\S\ref{sec:igvnd_safety}), and
the random half of the destruction step, which keeps the walk from anchoring
in one basin.  The remaining concessions to a certified optimum are two
loose-triangle seeds ($1.5$ and $8.5$ objective units), a residual of search
variance rather than of representation.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The two methods are complementary rather than competing.  The MILP is the
instrument of record on instances it can close: it certifies optimality on
the small and loosely constrained cases, and it is the yardstick that lets
us \emph{measure} the heuristic's quality there --- where IGVND is shown to
be exact or near-exact.  IGVND is the instrument of choice at operational
scale: on every configuration the MILP cannot close within the budget, the
heuristic delivers a markedly better feasible schedule in the same 60\,s,
with a guaranteed-feasible zero-movement floor and a checker-validated
manoeuvre-aware polish.  Read jointly, the MILP validates the heuristic on
the tractable end of the benchmark and the heuristic extends usable
solution quality to the intractable end --- which is the regime an
operator actually faces.
